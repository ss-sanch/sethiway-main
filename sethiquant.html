Options Pricing Architecture

This dynamic pricing engine automatically routes your parameters to the correct quantitative model based on the selected exercise style.

European (Black-Scholes): Options that can only be exercised on the exact expiration date. The engine uses the closed-form Black-Scholes-Merton differential equation to calculate the precise theoretical premium and the real-time Greek risk sensitivities.

American (Binomial Lattice): Options that can be exercised early at any point before expiration. The engine uses the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein (CRR) recursive tree, stepping backwards from maturity to calculate the absolute maximum value between holding the contract and exercising it early at every single node.

  • Implied Volatility (IV): The market's theoretical forecast of future price swings. Higher IV massively inflates option premiums. Note: When you enter a ticker, the engine automatically calculates and inputs the stock's Annualised Historical Volatility (based on the last 252 trading days) as a baseline. Historical volatility is backward-looking, whereas true IV is forward-looking and reverse-engineered from live order books. You can manually adjust this input to test different IV scenarios.
  • Risk-Free Rate: The theoretical baseline return with zero risk, typically pegged to government treasury bonds.
  • Lattice Steps: Only active for American options. Higher steps increase pricing accuracy but require exponentially more server calculus.

The Greeks (Risk Matrix)

  • Delta (Δ): Directional risk. Estimates how much the option's price will change for every $1 move in the underlying asset.
  • Gamma (Γ): Convexity risk. The rate of change in Delta for a $1 move in the underlying. Highest for at-the-money options.
  • Theta (Θ): Time decay. Measures how much theoretical value the option loses each day simply as time passes, assuming all else is equal.
  • Vega (V): Volatility risk. How much the option's premium will adjust for a 1% change in Implied Volatility.

Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT)

This engine uses SciPy's Sequential Least Squares Programming (SLSQP) algorithm to mathematically calculate the "Tangency Portfolio"—the exact asset allocation that maximizes risk-adjusted returns.

  • Sharpe Ratio: A measure of risk-adjusted return. Formula: $S = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_p}$. The optimizer runs thousands of simulations to find the highest possible ratio.
  • Expected Return: The weighted average of historical annualized returns.
  • Portfolio Volatility: The overall risk, calculated using the covariance matrix of the assets to account for diversification benefits.
  • Constraints: The algorithm strictly enforces that weights must sum to 100% and prohibits short-selling (no negative weights).

Algorithmic Backtesting

This module tests rules-based trading strategies against 5 years of historical market data. All signals are shifted by 1 day to strictly eliminate look-ahead bias (you cannot execute a trade at the closing price of the signal day).

Trading Strategies

SMA Crossover (Trend)

A momentum strategy. The algorithm generates a "Buy" signal when the Fast SMA crosses above the Slow SMA (Golden Cross), and sells into cash when it crosses below (Death Cross).

Bollinger Bands (Mean Reversion)

A volatility-based strategy. Calculates a 20-day SMA with bands at 2 standard deviations. The algorithm buys when the asset drops below the lower band (statistically oversold) and sells when it touches the upper band (overbought).

Risk Metrics

  • Max Drawdown: The largest peak-to-trough drop in the strategy's portfolio value. A critical measure of downside risk.
  • Annualised Volatility: The standard deviation of the strategy's daily returns, scaled to a 252-day trading year to measure the dispersion of returns.

Market Risk: Monte Carlo VaR

This engine models extreme portfolio tail-risk by running 10,000 distinct price simulations using Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM), a stochastic calculus model used by institutional trading desks to forecast asset behaviour.

  • Value at Risk (VaR): The maximum expected loss over a specific timeframe at a given confidence level. E.g., a 99% VaR of -10% means there is only a 1% probability the asset drops more than 10%.
  • Expected Shortfall (CVaR): While VaR tells you the threshold, CVaR tells you the average severity of the loss if that 1% worst-case scenario actually occurs.
  • Stochastic Shocks: The backend calculates 5 years of historical drift and volatility, applying random normal shocks ($Z$) to project 10,000 unique forward paths.
